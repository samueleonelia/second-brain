# Write for the Buyer Not the Feed Conversation-Starter Hooks

## The Story

Andy Mewborn's core content diagnosis is that most people write to look smart, not to get bought from. In his newsletter he frames the mistake bluntly: he was making content, but his buyers wanted conversations. A generic tips post — his example is "5 tips to improve your sales process" — earns likes and nothing else; a post that names a specific, expensive buyer situation makes the right person stop scrolling. His own illustration: a VP of Sales losing a $340K deal because of their demo deck. The stakes are quantified and role-specific, so the ideal buyer silently asks "wait, was it MY deck?" and reaches out. As he puts it: "One makes you look smart. The other makes buyers raise their hand."

The mechanism is a hook engineered for self-identification, then a path off-platform. Andy says every post is built "to book meetings with buyers. Not followers. Not likes. Buyers," and he reports outsized lead capture from it — by his own account "3,847 leads from ONE post," with even his weak posts clearing 5,000+ views (all figures his, stated in andysnewsletter.com). Those readers get funneled into his owned channel: a newsletter he pegs at 31,000+ subscribers. This isn't new theory for him — he credits the same buyer-first content with driving 30% of pipeline while he helped scale Outreach from $0 to roughly $250M ARR (his stated numbers).

The through-line to his other plays is deliberate. Because the post is written for a named buyer with a real problem, the reply — a DM, a booked call, a newsletter opt-in — is the whole point, not a byproduct. Reach that doesn't produce a raised hand, in Andy's model, is a vanity metric.

## Lesson for Creators

Write to the one buyer you want, not to the feed. Lead with a specific, quantified situation that person is living — a VP losing a six-figure deal because of their deck, say — so they self-identify and message you, instead of posting generic advice that collects likes from peers who'll never buy. Then give the raised hand somewhere to go — a lead magnet, a newsletter, a booked call — so attention converts into an owned audience rather than evaporating in the algorithm.
