# Invest in Your High Performers First

## The Story

When Peter Yang asked how to split time between strong and weak performers, Dave Kline led with a confession: "I screw this up I had this backwards." Early on he poured time into his low performers at the expense of his high performers, and stepping back to the pure math, that is a terrible trade. If a low performer produces at about 50% of an average employee and a top performer runs closer to 2x (the returns are rarely linear), then lifting the 50% person by 10% gets you a 55% person, while lifting the 2x person by the same 10% gets you two-and-a-half times an average employee. Pure logic says nearly every marginal minute should go to the high performers.

The trap is that the pull runs the other way. Low performers are the ones causing problems and making the most noise, so they demand your attention and quietly capture the time that would compound far more if spent on your best people. His prescription is discipline: do whatever it takes, in a deliberate way, to keep investing in your high performers. "Just skip my mistake and do that."

Kline is clear this is about time allocation, not writing off strugglers. He has a separate, careful process for low performers (first removing every environmental excuse, then separating will from skill). But the default gravitational pull toward the squeakiest wheel is exactly what a disciplined manager has to resist, because the highest-return investment is almost always the person already performing.

## Lesson for Creators

Your attention flows to whoever makes the most noise, which is usually your weakest performer, and that's backwards. Run the math: a small improvement on your best people compounds into far more output than the same effort spent rescuing a struggler. Build a deliberate habit that protects time for your top performers instead of letting the demanding ones absorb it by default.
