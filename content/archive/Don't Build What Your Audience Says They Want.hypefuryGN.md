# Don't Build What Your Audience Says They Want

## The Story

Lookout Media's readers tell Geoff Sharpe, over and over, that they come for the events. Events get the most clicks; surveys keep surfacing them. And yet Geoff deliberately does not build the product his audience asks for. On the Creator Spotlight podcast (2025) he explained the gap that drives the decision: _"what people are willing to pay for is very different from what they're willing to sort of say that they want."_

If he followed the stated demand, the outcome is easy to predict: _"if I only did what people told us to cover in terms of topics, it would be an event-based newsletter"_ and a real-estate newsletter. But nobody pays $100 a year for an events list — you can find events anywhere. What sustains Lookout's roughly 3,000 paying members, about 75% of revenue, is original hard journalism and deeper City Hall reporting that readers cannot easily get elsewhere. Events are the hook; the reporting is the thing worth paying for.

Geoff positions this as an editorial responsibility, not contrarianism for its own sake. He still runs surveys and watches engagement closely; he just treats stated preference as one input with guard rails, not the map. Part of running a media company, he argues, is _"giving an audience what they don't know that they want"_ — sparking interest in stories readers would never have requested. He places himself in the middle of a spectrum: traditional outlets ignore their audience entirely, pure audience-chasers get captured by it, and Lookout listens for the guard rails while keeping the editorial call.

## Lesson for Creators

Clicks and survey answers measure what people will consume for free, not what they will pay for — and those are often different products. If you monetize through memberships, optimize the paid core around revealed willingness-to-pay, even when your most-engaged content points somewhere cheaper. Use the popular, low-value material (events, listicles) as the top-of-funnel hook, and reserve the paywall for the work only you can produce. Listening to your audience means finding the guard rails, not handing them the editorial pen.
