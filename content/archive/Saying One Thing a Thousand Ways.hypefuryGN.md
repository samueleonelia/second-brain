# Saying One Thing a Thousand Ways

## The Story

Adam Robinson frames consistent LinkedIn posting as doing what a PR agency did for a public figure fifty years ago: finding as many different ways to tell the same story about one person until it lodges in the collective consciousness. In LinkedIn parlance he calls these content pillars, and he strips the idea to its core: _"All a content pillar is is you just saying the same thing over and over again in many different ways"_ (Ahrefs Podcast).

He did not know this when he started. He internalized it on a 16-hour solo drive from Aspen to Austin, studying Chris Walker, a marketing creator he openly modeled. On that drive he realized Walker was, in his words, literally saying the same handful of things over and over, and had been for years, yet made every post feel new. That was the unlock. As Adam puts it, _"the skill is saying it in a way where people do not realize that that is what you're doing"_ (Ahrefs Podcast).

The mechanic has three moving parts. First, the pillars must be authentic to you, because the posts that break through are polarizing and 10-20% of readers will hate you for them; you have to be willing to defend that hill. Second, they must resonate with the market, which is the content-market-fit search: you post for months, something suddenly gets a hundred times the usual engagement, you rebuild that idea in a different form, and if it hits again you have found a pillar. Third, they must be narrow. Adam's own pillars are bootstrapped SaaS, future go-to-market, bootstrapping-versus-VC, and building in public, so much so that he notes hardly anyone who follows him knows he has two kids, because he has deliberately never made family a pillar.

The result is a manufactured but true identity: ask anyone who follows Adam who he is, and they will describe the same person he engineered through a thousand posts.

## Lesson for Creators

Fear of repetition is the enemy. Because only a fraction of your followers see any given post, and because they forget, the same idea has to be said again and again, just dressed differently. Choose two or three specific, defensible themes, not the safe mainstream ones, and hammer each from new angles. The craft is not variety of topic; it is variety of expression around a fixed, narrow message.
