# The Delegation Ladder

## The Story

Dave Kline argues that leaders who won't delegate are almost always afraid of something: afraid of drifting from the craft that gives them their identity (he saw this constantly with proud software developers), or afraid the person they hand work to won't do it as well. Those fears are usually true in the near term but hold you back in the medium to long term, and a manager's whole job is to get the right people doing the right work in a high-leverage way. If you never get over the fears, he says, you never actually become a manager.

His fix is a sequence rather than a leap. Do not start by handing off the critical thing you are best at. Start with the lowest-risk work: things fully inside your team where you are the one who decides whether to react, so nothing touches customers or other departments. Next add anything carrying the experiment tag (Skunk Works, beta projects) because that work already carries the expectation it might go poorly, so the downside of delegating it is small.

On the people side he inverts the instinct too. "Don't take something that you have a superpower in and give that away." Instead pick the tasks you struggle with, the ones you constantly put off, and hand them to someone on your team who is different from you and will be energized by exactly that work, so your grind turns into their growth. Only after moving your weaknesses and the experimental, internal work do you start giving away the critical things you are best at.

He frames "delegate everything" as aspirational: as you offload, you free space to think strategically and take on more, which means more lands on your plate to delegate again. The recurring mistake is not delegating too soon but too late. Hire competent people, empower them, and more often than not they won't screw it up, and many will do the work better than you did.

## Lesson for Creators

Delegation stalls on fear, so lower the stakes of the first step. Offload low-risk, internal, and clearly experimental work first, and give away your weaknesses (to people wired for them) before your strengths. That builds the muscle and the trust to eventually hand off even your best work. The default error is waiting too long, not letting go too early.
