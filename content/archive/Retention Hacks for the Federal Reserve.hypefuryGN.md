# Retention Hacks for the Federal Reserve

## The Story

In her 20-minute video on the leaked MrBeast production memo, Kyla Scanlon stops mid-argument to indict herself. After laying out how MrBeast studies retention graphs to make each video more addictive, she admits she runs the same playbook on monetary policy: _"I think about attention spans when I make videos I do hacks to keep people listening about the Federal Reserve"_.

The confession is the mechanism. Scanlon does not treat educational content as exempt from the attention economy. Skits where she plays Jerome Powell, whiteboard videos that break the talking-head monotony, poem-structured scripts, format changes that reset the viewer's attention: each is a retention device aimed at material that would otherwise lose to everything else on the feed. Her framing of the competition is mechanical, not moral: _"you're fighting against the entire world for just a second of people's time"_.

What separates her use of the playbook from the one she criticizes is what the retention is spent on. MrBeast, in her analysis, optimizes retention as the end product, with meaning lost in the metrics. She spends the same engineered seconds delivering the Fed's dual mandate to someone who would never read a Fed statement. The tooling is identical, the payload is different.

## Lesson for Creators

Educational creators routinely lose to entertainment because they treat retention engineering as beneath the material. Scanlon's position is that the dry topic is precisely why the hacks are required: the harder the subject, the more deliberately each video has to earn its next second. Study retention like an entertainer, then spend the attention on substance.
