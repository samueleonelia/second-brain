# Align on the How, Not Just the What

## The Story

Asked to name the single highest-return move for any manager at any level, Kline says it is to "co-author and align on clear expectations." Most disagreements and disappointments, he argues, come from a leader holding expectations they think are obvious but never communicate, while a well-meaning, talented person does common-sense things and arrives somewhere completely different. They were both trying; they just ran a different race.

His sharpest distinction is that "the how is more important than the what." Setting the what (a metric, a milestone, a target like growing your territory by $10 million) is not profound, and almost everyone can do it. What people skip is the how: the cultural norms, budget constraints, and system that turn a goal into coordinated action. He illustrates it with soccer. Five-year-olds get a crystal-clear what (put the ball in that goal) and produce beehive soccer: kids swarming the ball in a big pack. Man City has the identical what, but Pep Guardiola divides the field into channels and lanes, limits how many players sit in each, and orbits the whole system around getting the ball into a tiny high-expected-goal zone as many times as possible. Same what, but a shared how, so the players know their position, anticipate each other, and optimize the process metric that produces goals.

He makes it concrete with a sales team. Rather than stop at a territory number, a two-minute conversation aligns the how: are we focused on retention or new accounts; are we doing that through cold outreach, referrals, or social; and if the head of sales doesn't yet know, the agreed how becomes a set of experiments, with a check-in in three months on which failed, which won, and where to double down. In two minutes both sides now know exactly what to expect and can hold mutual accountability, instead of pursuing divergent strategies and colliding on the back end with a lot more emotion.

## Lesson for Creators

Handing someone a goal number is not delegation; it's the setup for a collision. Spend the two minutes to co-author the how (the system, constraints, and the experiments that will find the path) so both sides share the same picture before work starts. Clear expectations up front, with almost no emotion, prevent the messy back-end reckoning where the real friction lives.
