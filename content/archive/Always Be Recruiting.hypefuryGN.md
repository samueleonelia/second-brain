# Always Be Recruiting

## The Story

Dave Kline says it took him roughly 15 or 16 years to learn the difference between hiring and recruiting. Hiring is reactive: a seat opens, you send HR three to five bullet points, they post it on LinkedIn, and they hand you the best available candidate at that moment, which he warns may well be relatively mediocre. Recruiting is the opposite. You imagine your world 6 to 12 months out, name the roles you will probably need, and ask who the best people in the world for those roles are if money and location were no object. Then you build relationships with that short list before you need them, so when the seat opens you are choosing from warm, pre-vetted people who are not on the market and not being fought over by other companies. Same total effort, dripped out slowly, produces a better hire at lower cost.

He can name the exact moment he saw it. He was at Madison Square Garden with colleagues, including his boss, when a beer vendor came by. His boss walked away with the vendor's phone number, and when Kline asked what that was about, the boss said "always be recruiting." Over the next 18 months that boss nurtured the relationship with the Madison Square Garden beer vendor, who then became his chief of staff for the next seven years.

To show recruiting is a discipline, not a lucky break, Kline points to the top-grading interview literature and the story of the CEO of Aon, a multi-billion-dollar consulting firm. That CEO expects to personally recruit about 30 people into the company every year. Because his hit rate is roughly 1 in 10, he keeps a warm nurture list of about 300 people, and he works it in about 90 minutes a week, making sure he reconnects meaningfully with each name roughly once a quarter with an article, a ping, or a note (numbers per Kline in the Tom Hunt interview). Kline's point: if the CEO of a company that size spends a quarter of his effort on 300 relationships, a five- or fifty-person founder should be doing it more, not less.

## Lesson for Creators

Do not wait for the vacancy. Keep a running short list of the people you would most want, from collaborators to hires, and touch each of them on a light cadence long before you have an opening. When you finally need someone, you are picking from warm, pre-qualified people instead of scrambling for whoever is available and on the market. Recruiting is a slow drip you control; hiring is a panic you inherit.
