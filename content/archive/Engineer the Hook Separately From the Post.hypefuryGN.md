# Engineer the Hook Separately From the Post

## The Story

Aakash Gupta learned early that effort spent on a post and effort spent on its hook are two different budgets. He can pour ten hours into a piece and ten seconds into the opening line and watch it die, or spend ten minutes on the piece and serious time on the hook and watch it carry. So he reallocated: even a ten-hour post now gets roughly an hour devoted to the hook alone, because the hook is what buys the distribution that makes the ten hours worth anything.

His process treats the hook as a testable component, not a flourish. He drafts the whole post around one candidate hook, then asks three separate AI models for ten one-line hooks each, built on strong copywriting principles and aimed at getting readers to tap show-more. From those thirty he keeps a few, rewords them, and narrows to two finalists. Because the hook also dictates the second line and the post's structure, he lays the post out under each finalist to see which one the body actually supports. Then he previews the winner across mobile, desktop, and tablet in a Chrome extension so he knows exactly what shows above the fold before anyone taps. After publishing, he reads the first ten minutes of performance as a live verdict and, if the hook is not landing, swaps in a backup he kept in reserve.

The discipline is in refusing to let a strong post ship behind a weak opening. He admits he has not mastered the hook, which is precisely why he systematizes it rather than trusting instinct.

## Lesson for Creators

Budget for the hook as its own deliverable, because it controls whether the work behind it ever gets seen. The copyable system is concrete: generate many hook variants, narrow to two, preview them where readers actually read, and let the first minutes of real performance, not your taste, pick the winner. The best post in the world fails behind a line nobody taps.
