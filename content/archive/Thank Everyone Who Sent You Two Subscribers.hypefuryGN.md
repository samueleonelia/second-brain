# Thank Everyone Who Sent You Two Subscribers

## The Story

In *Growth in Reverse*'s early days, Chenell Basilio had SparkLoop connected to her newsletter, which let her see exactly where new subscribers were coming from. Most creators glance at that dashboard and move on. Chenell used it as a prospecting list for gratitude. When she saw a referral, she traced it back: "Oh, two subscribers came from this person." Then she went and found the actual email where that person had shared her link and replied to it directly: "Thank you so much. I really appreciate you sharing."

The size of the referral was beside the point. Her own reasoning was that even a tiny share was a real gift: "they only sent two subscribers, but that's a huge deal," because someone had taken the time to put her work in front of their own audience. So she thanked all of them, one by one, in the early days when the numbers were small enough to do it by hand.

The second-order effect was reputational. The personal reply signaled she was paying attention as a person, not running a growth machine: it "really stood out to people of like, oh, this isn't just like a game like she actually cares." Those acknowledged referrers are exactly the people who share again and who become the peer relationships every newsletter creator eventually leans on. Chenell frames relationship-building as one of the few universal patterns she found across every creator she studied: "all of them have built good relationships with other creators." Her thank-you habit is the low-tech, do-things-that-don't-scale version of building that web from zero.

## Lesson for Creators

Referral attribution isn't just a metric to report, it's a list of people who already helped you and are primed to help again. The move is to close the loop manually while you still can: find the specific person behind each referral and thank them personally, especially the small ones, because the smallness is what makes the gesture surprising. It costs minutes, it compounds into repeat shares and genuine relationships, and it's precisely the kind of human touch that automation makes rarer and therefore more memorable.
