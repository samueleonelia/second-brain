# Spend 50 Hours on One Deep Dive Nobody Else Would

## The Story

Chenell Basilio started *Growth in Reverse* in December 2022 without a growth plan. She had stumbled on Mario Gabriele, who writes *The Generalist*, and heard he had made $300K in his first year of writing a newsletter. She could not let it go: she wanted to know exactly how. So she went to work reverse-engineering him, and by her own account "spent like 50 or 60 hours just digging into how he did it" for that single piece, reading all his past Twitter threads and every podcast interview he had done, then compiling roughly a 3,000-word deep dive stuffed with screenshots. She hit publish mostly to see if anyone else wanted to read it.

That became the format. Every week she reverse-engineered a different creator who had grown a newsletter to 50,000+ subscribers, and she put in the same absurd depth: "I'd spend like 30 to 40 hours researching how a creator grew to 50,000 subscribers or more." Her own framing of why it worked is blunt: it "was unique. People hadn't seen someone like spend that amount of time on a specific article." In a niche full of quick tactical tips, the sheer research load was the differentiator, and the depth is what made each issue shareable and worth staying subscribed for.

The research itself is a repeatable process, not magic. She starts from wherever the subject actually creates ("wherever they really create content is where I start"), builds a playlist of every podcast the person has done, and does the listening on foot: "I go on four to five mile walks and I'll try and get as many listen to as I can taking notes while I walk." Then she sits down mid-week to compile, runs Twitter advanced searches for any mention of subscriber counts or newsletter URLs, and digs until the primary sources run out, sometimes going as deep as the tenth or eleventh page of Google results. She keeps notes in Apple Notes, not an elaborate system.

She is also disciplined about which subjects earn a deep dive. If the growth story isn't "compelling or different or replicable," she kills it, because the whole point is a mechanism a reader can actually copy. The result was fast: by roughly the six-month mark she told Josh Spector she had "just hit 12,000" subscribers, and about two and a half years in she was up to roughly 40,000.

## Lesson for Creators

The moat here is effort other people won't spend. When a niche is saturated with the same recycled tips, out-researching everyone is a real differentiation strategy: one obsessively deep, primary-sourced piece a week can out-perform ten shallow ones because it is genuinely unavailable elsewhere and worth forwarding. The transferable process is concrete: start where the subject actually publishes, batch the input (podcast playlists on walks), mine primary sources for the hard numbers, and refuse to publish a story that isn't replicable for the reader.
