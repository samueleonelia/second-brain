# The Ten-Person Commenting Play

## The Story

Asked where a beginner with limited time should start on LinkedIn, Adam Robinson does not point at posting. He points at commenting. His instruction is specific and small: make a list of the influential people in your ecosystem and thoughtfully comment on their posts, in a way that is obviously not AI-generated. He is scathing about the alternative; he can spot the AI-comment crowd instantly and finds it worthless.

The mechanic is deliberately unscalable. He describes taking the time to write a meaningful reply to people who commented on his own posts, treating it as the same personal-connection engine at scale that his raw video does. And he frames the beginner version as relationship arbitrage: _"if you have a list of 10 people in your space and you spend a few days a week commenting on their stuff they might start commenting on your stuff"_ (Maja Voje, GTM Strategist Podcast). Do it for several months and you form real relationships with those people, they start seeing your work, and you slowly become known in the ecosystem.

There is an algorithmic reason it compounds, and it is counterintuitive. The one signal Adam says he has heard LinkedIn values, indirectly, is not comments from your first-degree network. It is _"second and third degree connections commenting on your post is what it likes more than anything"_ (Ahrefs Podcast), essentially engagement from people who don't already know you. First-degree comments might just be friends being nice; strangers commenting is what tells the algorithm to spread the post. Commenting your way into new networks is how you earn that second- and third-degree reach in the first place.

## Lesson for Creators

Before you optimize your own posts, spend your engagement budget on ten people whose audience you want. Real comments, over months, not automated ones. It builds relationships that turn into reciprocal reach, and it feeds the exact signal, engagement from outside your immediate network, that the platform amplifies. The slow, manual, ten-name version beats broadcasting into a network that cannot yet see you.
