# The I'll Just Do It Trap

## The Story

Dave Kline names the notorious type-A manager reflex as the statement "that's okay I'll do it", and he flags three ways it's dangerous. First, the reassurance is usually a lie: you're quietly taking back work that fell short, so the report never hears that they missed the deadline or the mark. Second, taking it back undoes the entire system you invested in, the whole point of setting expectations, delegating, and hiring people more capable than you in certain areas. Third, doing it yourself kills the compounding: you could have coached, trained, or given feedback to keep the work where it belongs, but when you just do it, the person learns nothing.

The cousin to that reflex is the famous Harvard monkey case study, where your team secretly delegates work back up to you. Someone drops a draft proposal on your desk just to get your eyes on it, you say leave it with me, and now you are the bottleneck; if you don't respond in a day, they start managing you, chasing the status. Kline's rule is to catch it early: "when you see the monkey coming you got to keep it with them." The easy way to say no is to ask "what do you propose" and then probe: do you actually need me to review this, is there a specific point you need me to engage on, or how much damage could we do if you just sent it? That keeps the work with the owner.

Kline notes the asymmetry with a live example from the interview: the host had let a 40-page contract land on his desk with a quick promise to take a look, turning a 20-30 minute task into an hour of surrounding friction plus low-burning anxiety, while the correct 20-second response would have been to ask the report to highlight the exact language they were stuck on and say what they'd recommend if the manager didn't exist.

## Lesson for Creators

Taking the work back feels generous and is actually lose-lose: no feedback for them, no leverage for you. When work boomerangs back, don't accept the monkey; hand it back with "what do you propose" and a request to name the specific decision they need from you. A 20-second question beats a 20-minute rescue every time, and it's the only version where the other person actually grows.
