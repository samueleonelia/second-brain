# Start Posting When Your Niche Goes Wacky

## The Story

Kyla Scanlon published her first TikTok around August 2020, but the channel only became a consistent bet in December 2020. The date was not arbitrary. As she told Bloomberg in a 2022 interview, she started making TikToks right around when GameStop started happening, just after she had left her buy-side job at Capital Group, which meant she was no longer under compliance restrictions that barred her from public market commentary. The markets were so strange in that window that, in her telling, it was almost impossible not to make content about them.

Two constraints lifted at the same moment. Leaving the employer removed the legal muzzle. The GameStop mania created a flood of new retail traders who suddenly needed someone to explain options, short squeezes, and the Fed in plain language. She walked into that demand spike with finance training, a stock blog's worth of writing reps, and little competition from licensed professionals who were still compliance-bound.

From that December 2020 start, the growth compounded across platforms: TikTok first, then Twitter, YouTube, Instagram, and a newsletter that passed 57,000 subscribers within three years.

## Lesson for Creators

Cold starts are cheapest when the niche is in chaos, because chaos manufactures an audience of confused newcomers faster than incumbents can serve them. The replicable move is to watch for the moment your topic hits mainstream confusion and to clear your publishing constraints before it arrives, so the launch can ride the spike instead of preparing through it.
