# Channel-Offer Fit Beats Product-Market Fit

## The Story

During his Bootstrap MBA year Jakob Greenfeld kept running into the term product-market fit and concluded it was a concept mainly useful to VCs evaluating investments, not to a founder deciding what to do on Monday. It describes a state, not a roadmap; the words product and market are too vague to act on. What he replaced it with, as he explained in the Indie MBA conversation, are two concrete puzzle pieces per project: "what's the channel what's the offer".

The channel side is empirical, not theoretical. He runs every product through its own exploration: "I have just my list of channels you can explore and you never know what works". Gut feeling picks the starting point, results decide. Across his portfolio the outcomes diverge wildly by product: he has a product "where Google ads are working amazingly well", he sees success with newsletter ads for different products, and Reddit ads work for him at limited scale. The prize for finding a paid channel that converts is a growth loop that needs no audience at all: if you can "make a dollar twenty for every dollar you spend", you keep spending and it keeps growing.

The offer side is the other half of the same experiment, and it is more than price: which features are included, what guarantees you give, how fast you deliver. The interactions are counterintuitive; he has had sales completely drop after lowering a price, because a too-cheap offer reads as untrustworthy. Get one variable wrong and the combination fails, which is exactly why he prefers a framing that names the variables over one that does not.

He is also frank about the cultural bias this fights: the never-paid-for-ads story is celebrated because it is attractive, and it is heavily over-represented among the tiny minority who won the organic game early. Most working entrepreneurs pay for distribution because it works and it frees them to spend their time on the product and the customers instead of on the influencer game.

## Lesson for Creators

Audience growth and product growth stop being mysterious when you decompose them into a channel variable and an offer variable and iterate both. Keep a standing list of channels, give each new product its own test sequence, and treat pricing, guarantees and delivery speed as experiments rather than decisions. The organic-only path is a real option but a statistically rare one; a channel-offer pair that returns more than it costs is a growth engine you own regardless of follower count.
