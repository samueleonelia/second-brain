# Micro-Lessons Sized to the LinkedIn Feed

## The Story

Eddie Shleyner's signature unit is the *micro-lesson*: a short copywriting lesson wrapped in a story. What made it a growth engine rather than just a nice format is that he built it to fit one specific distribution surface exactly. When he describes his process, the platform constraint is baked into the writing itself. A random anecdote gets fused to a single copywriting principle, and the whole thing has to land inside LinkedIn's post limit. In his own words, "i want to keep them in that like word count you know it's like 1300 characters so you're probably gonna end up with like 220 words." The constraint is not applied at publish time. It is the container he writes into from the first draft.

Each micro-lesson runs the same internal skeleton: a headline that makes a promise, a lead that drops the reader into the middle of a story (he uses the *in media res* technique), a bridge to the copywriting principle, and a call to action. That fixed structure is what lets him produce them fast and consistently while keeping every one self-contained. A reader gets a complete idea in 220 words, which is exactly what performs in a scrolling feed.

Because the format was designed for the medium, reposting cost almost nothing: the same micro-article that lived on VeryGoodCopy.com dropped straight into a LinkedIn post without being rewritten to fit. As he puts it, "i like to like repost them on linkedin," and sizing to the feed is what makes that repost frictionless. The choice of LinkedIn over Twitter was itself a format decision. A few-hundred-word lesson had room to breathe on LinkedIn in a way it did not on Twitter's shorter native format. Per Growth In Reverse's profile, his LinkedIn presence of roughly 110,000 to 119,000 followers dwarfs his 14,000 on Twitter.

The payoff compounded. VeryGoodCopy grew into a widely read newsletter, and his own site cites over 1 million monthly content views and LinkedIn Top Voice status in Marketing and Advertising. The lesson unit and the platform were co-designed, and that fit is why the format became the recognizable product.

## Lesson for Creators

Pick the surface you actually want to grow on, learn its exact native constraints (character count, structure, what the feed rewards), and design your content unit to fit those constraints before you write, not after. A piece engineered to the platform's size posts natively with zero reformatting, which is what makes consistent distribution sustainable. The constraint is not a limit on the idea. It is the mold that makes the idea shippable and repeatable.
