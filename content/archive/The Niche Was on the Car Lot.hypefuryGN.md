# The Niche Was on the Car Lot

## The Story

Asked by Philip DeFranco what drew her to economics as a career, Kyla Scanlon pointed to a sales floor, not a lecture hall: _"I used to sell cars in college and people would come in and not know what an interest rate was"_. She was studying finance, economics, and data analytics at Western Kentucky at the time, and the daily collision between her coursework and her customers read to her as plainly unfair. People were signing five-figure loans without the vocabulary to evaluate them.

The car lot functioned as accidental customer discovery. It told her exactly who the audience was (ordinary buyers, not finance professionals), exactly what they lacked (the basic toolkit, starting with interest rates), and exactly how mainstream finance content failed them (it assumed the toolkit). DeFranco, whose father sold cars, confirmed the brutality of the finance office from the other side: buyers walked out thinking they got a deal when they had not.

She then went to the buy side at Capital Group, graduated into the pandemic, and chose financial education as the answer to what she wanted to do with, in her words, this one life. The niche was never brainstormed. It was witnessed, years before she made her first video.

## Lesson for Creators

The strongest niche selection data comes from jobs that put you face to face with the audience's ignorance, not from keyword tools. A day job where customers repeatedly fail at the same thing is a recorded list of content that needs to exist. The replicable move is to mine your own service-job history for the question customers kept getting wrong, and make that question the channel.
