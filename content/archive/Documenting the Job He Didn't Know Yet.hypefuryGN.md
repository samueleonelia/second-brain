# Documenting the Job He Didn't Know Yet

## The Story

VeryGoodCopy did not start as an audience play. Eddie Shleyner had been hired as a copywriter without knowing much about copywriting, so he set himself a rule: whenever he learned something useful, from a podcast, a book, a seminar, or an old direct-response master, he would force himself to write it up. The test was pedagogical, not promotional. If he could teach a principle clearly, he knew it well enough to use it in his own work. He kept these write-ups in a running Google Doc, dozens of them, long before any of it was public. In the interview archived by Growth In Reverse he is explicit that he did not want an audience at all, and that the goal was simply to get better at the job he had been given.

He states the mechanism plainly in his Growth Memo interview. "that's why i started very good copy," he says, because "i needed some way to put what i've learned in a box and just like quantify it to myself." The writing was a knowledge-verification device first and a content asset second. That ordering is what produced a deep, non-obvious library. He was documenting real, hard-won lessons, not manufacturing content to a posting schedule.

He frames the general principle the way Gary Vaynerchuk does, capture rather than invent: "one of the best ways to create a lot of good content document not don't create." His argument is that if you document your actual journey you stay authentic and you compound your own learning at the same time. By the time a coworker suggested he put the doc online, he had a stockpile. Growth In Reverse's profile puts it at roughly 50 to 60 lessons already written, so the newsletter launched with a backlog, not a blank page.

The through-line: the audience was a byproduct of a private learning practice. Because the content began as genuine self-education, it carried an authority that content-for-content's-sake never earns, and it gave him a running start the day he decided to publish.

## Lesson for Creators

If you're early and you don't yet have expertise to broadcast, don't wait until you're an expert. Document the learning itself. Write up each thing you figure out as if you had to teach it, and keep the notes in one place. Two things happen. You actually learn faster, because teaching forces real understanding, and you quietly accumulate a backlog of genuine, specific content. When you're ready to publish, you launch from a stockpile of hard-won material instead of a cold start, and the work reads as authentic because it is your real journey, not a performance of one.
