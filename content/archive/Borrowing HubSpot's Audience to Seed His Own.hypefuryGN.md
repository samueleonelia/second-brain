# Borrowing HubSpot's Audience to Seed His Own

## The Story

When Eddie Shleyner started publishing, he had no audience. His first real distribution did not come from his own site. It came from HubSpot's. According to Growth In Reverse's profile, he cold-approached a HubSpot editor in person at their Inbound conference, recognized her, walked up, and simply asked whether he could submit something. She said yes. He spent a long time on the piece, tried to do a genuinely good job, and they published it.

That first guest post went live on HubSpot in December 2015, a piece titled *The Kurt Vonnegut Guide to Great Copywriting*. It landed well, but as Growth In Reverse notes, it was still on someone else's website, doing nothing directly for him. He kept going and wrote more than sixteen articles for HubSpot over the following years. The pivotal move came with his third guest post, in February 2016, when he included a backlink to VeryGoodCopy inside the article. That single post drove over 1,000 visitors to his own site at roughly a 20 percent conversion rate, about 200 signups from one borrowed placement. Across all his HubSpot writing, Growth In Reverse estimates he pulled somewhere in the range of 3,000 to 5,000 newsletter subscribers.

The most telling detail is what happened before he even had a subscribe mechanism. HubSpot readers who liked his work went looking for more, arrived at his site, and found no email form, so they filled out his contact form asking how to join his list. Demand for the newsletter existed before the newsletter did, manufactured entirely by publishing on a platform that already had the readers he wanted.

The play is a classic borrowed-audience cold start. He did not wait to build reach. He went to where a large, relevant audience already gathered, earned a spot by doing the work well, and then quietly installed a path from their platform back to something he owned.

## Lesson for Creators

When you have no audience, do not start by shouting into an empty room. Find the big platforms that already hold the readers you want, and get published on them, even if you have to pitch an editor in person and write for free at first. Then, once you are trusted enough to publish there, add one clear path back to something you own (a backlink, a mention, a signup). The borrowed platform supplies the traffic. Your job is to make sure a fraction of it converts into an audience that is yours, not rented.

The order matters: earn the placement first, prove the work, then insert the path home. A backlink in your very first pitch reads as self-serving. A backlink in your third, after you have delivered value twice, reads as earned.
